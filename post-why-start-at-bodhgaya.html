Why we always start at Bodhgaya — and end there too

Twenty-five years of running the India circuit, and the route still bends back to the Mahabodhi at the close. A note on why the same courtyard feels like a different place on the way out.

Written by
Ven. Tenzin Norbu
Role
Lead guide · India circuit
Published
24 January 2026
TN
Ven. Tenzin Norbu
Lead guide · India circuit · 24 January 2026

People sometimes ask why our India circuit is shaped the way it is. Chronologically, a pilgrimage "ought" to start at Lumbini, where the Buddha was born, and proceed to Kushinagar, where he died — birth to death, in order. We do it differently. We start at Bodhgaya, the place of the awakening, spend the whole journey looping out through the life, and then come back to Bodhgaya at the end. After twenty-five years I am more convinced of this shape than ever.

Start where it became possible

The reason is simple: Bodhgaya is where the man became the Buddha. Lumbini is where a child was born who might become anything; Bodhgaya is where he became the one whose birth we now honour. To begin at the awakening is to begin at the moment that makes all the other moments matter. Everything we visit afterward — the first teaching at Sarnath, the years of wandering, the death at Kushinagar — flows out of the forty-nine days under the tree. So we plant the group there first, for three nights, and let the awakening become the ground they stand on for the whole circuit.

Lumbini is where a child was born who might become anything. Bodhgaya is where he became the Buddha.

The loop of a life

From Bodhgaya we go out into the life — Rajgir and the teaching years, Nalanda and the scholarship that grew from it, Vaishali and the announcement of the end, Kushinagar and the death, Lumbini and, out of sequence, the birth, then Sarnath and the first turning of the wheel. It is not chronological. It is centrifugal: we spiral outward from the centre and then spiral back. By the time we return, the group has walked the whole arc — birth, teaching, community, death — and carries all of it.

The same courtyard, the last morning

And here is the thing I most want to describe, the thing that keeps the route as it is. On the first morning, Bodhgaya is overwhelming: enormous, foreign, crowded, a swirl of colour and language and unfamiliar devotion. Pilgrims are still tourists then, however sincere. They photograph the tree.

Two weeks later, after Kushinagar has taught them about death and Sarnath about the teaching and Lumbini about the beginning, we come back to the same courtyard. And every year, without fail, it is a different place. The crowd is the same; the tree is the same; the pilgrims are not. They do not photograph the tree the second time. They sit under it. Several always weep, and cannot quite say why, and I do not try to make them say. They have walked out into a life and come home to the place where it became possible, and they are no longer the people who arrived.

Why the shape holds

That is why the route bends back. A pilgrimage is not a line from birth to death; we have all been walking that line since we were born, and we do not need to fly to Bihar to do it. A pilgrimage is a circle that returns you, changed, to where you started, so that you can finally see it. We start at Bodhgaya because it is the centre. We end at Bodhgaya because only at the end can you understand the beginning. The tree does not move. You do.
