The first sermon at Sarnath, in five readings

The Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta in the Pali, two Tibetan commentaries, a Burmese chant transcription, and a 21st-century English. What changes across the readings; what refuses to.

Written by
Aiko Tanaka-roshi
Role
Lead guide · Zen Japan
Published
2 April 2026
AT
Aiko Tanaka-roshi
Lead guide · Zen Japan · 2 April 2026

At Sarnath, in the deer park, the Buddha said the thing he had waited seven weeks to say. We call it the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta — the discourse on the turning of the wheel of dharma — and it is, by common agreement, the first teaching. On our circuit we read it aloud, sitting on the grass beside the Dhamek Stupa, in five versions. Here is what I have learned from doing that, year after year.

1 · The Pali

The Pali is spare and structural. It moves like a legal document: here are two extremes to avoid, here is the middle way, here are four truths, here is each truth stated, then stated again as something to be understood, then a third time as something that has been understood. The famous phrase — cakkhuṃ udapādi, ñāṇaṃ udapādi, "vision arose, knowledge arose" — repeats twelve times, a wheel turning through its spokes. Read aloud, it is less a sermon than a machine for producing certainty.

2 · The first Tibetan commentary

The Tibetan scholastic tradition cannot leave a list of four alone; it must anatomise. The commentary I carry spends pages on a single question: in what sense is craving the origin of suffering, rather than merely a companion to it? The text of the sutta takes one line to say it. The commentary takes forty. Something is gained — precision, defensibility — and something is lost: the plainness of a man telling five old friends what he has found.

3 · The second Tibetan commentary

The second, later commentary is warmer, more meditative, less interested in winning arguments. It reads the four truths as a physician's sequence — diagnosis, cause, prognosis, treatment — and lingers on the good news hidden in the third truth: that suffering has an end, that this is a teaching about the possibility of freedom before it is a teaching about pain.

The four truths are usually taught as bad news. The third one is the best news anyone has ever given.

4 · The Burmese chant

The Burmese transcription is not for reading; it is for chanting, and it changes the sutta completely. Set to the long, rising-and-falling cadence of Burmese recitation, the repetitions stop being redundant and become musical — the twelvefold wheel turns as sound. A pilgrim who cannot follow a word of Pali will often say the chant moved her more than any translation. The meaning was never only in the meaning.

5 · The modern English

And then the plain contemporary English, which strips the honorifics and the archaisms and tries to let a person in 2026 hear what five ascetics heard in 528 BCE. It is the easiest to understand and the easiest to forget — precisely because it costs nothing to read. The older versions make you work, and the working is part of the receiving.

What does not change

Across all five, one thing holds perfectly still: the structure of the turning itself. Two extremes, a middle, four truths, each seen three times. You can render it as scripture, scholasticism, medicine, music or journalism, and the wheel is the same wheel. That, sitting in the deer park where it was first set spinning, is the thing I most want pilgrims to feel — that beneath every tradition's dressing of it, the first teaching is one shape, and it has not moved in two and a half thousand years.
