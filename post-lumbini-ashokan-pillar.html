Lumbini's Ashokan pillar: what the inscription actually says

“Here the Buddha was born.” Or so the pillar is usually paraphrased. We sat with the epigrapher who re-read it, and walked the garden with her notes — the real text is stranger and better.

Written by
Lobzang Wangchuk

Lead guide · Himalaya
Published

Everyone who visits Lumbini is told the same thing: that the Ashokan pillar beside the Maya Devi Temple bears an inscription reading "Here the Buddha was born," and that this is how we know the site is genuine. Both halves of that sentence are true, and both are more interesting than the paraphrase suggests. Last spring I walked the garden with an epigrapher who has spent her career with the Brahmi script, and she taught me to read the stone again.

The pillar and the moment

In 249 BCE — the twentieth year of his reign, by his own reckoning — the emperor Ashoka came to Lumbini on pilgrimage. He had, some years before, turned from conquest to the dharma after the horror of his war in Kalinga, and he spent the rest of his life marking the Buddhist landscape with polished sandstone pillars and edicts carved in the common speech of the people. At Lumbini he raised a pillar and had it inscribed. Then it stood for two thousand years, fell, was buried, and was rediscovered in 1896 by the archaeologist Alois Führer and the Nepali governor Khadga Shumsher — the find that ended centuries of uncertainty about where the Buddha was born.

The five lines

The inscription is not eight words. It is five lines of Brahmi, and paraphrased fully it says something like this: that King Piyadasi, beloved of the gods — Ashoka's title — came in person and worshipped here, because here the Buddha, the sage of the Shakyas, was born. That he had a stone wall built and a stone pillar raised. And — this is the part the paraphrase drops — that because the Blessed One was born here, he made the village of Lumbini free of taxes on religious grounds and liable to pay only one-eighth of its produce.

The oldest thing we can prove about the Buddha's birthplace is a tax exemption.

Why the tax line matters

My epigrapher friend loves that last clause more than the rest, and by the end of the day so did I. Anyone can carve piety into stone. But a specific administrative concession — this village, this rate, one-eighth — is the kind of thing a real government records because it really happened. It is the detail that turns a devotional claim into a historical fact. Ashoka was not only venerating the birthplace; he was reducing the burden on the people who lived at it, and filing the paperwork in stone.

Standing at the pillar

What I try to give pilgrims, walking them to the pillar at the end of the Lumbini morning, is this doubled feeling: the devotion and the receipt. On one side, an emperor kneeling at a village in the Terai because a child was born there three centuries earlier. On the other, the flat, factual voice of an administration recording a tax rate — the earliest hard evidence, older than any temple, that the story is anchored to this exact patch of ground. The two together are stronger than either alone. Faith says the Buddha was born here. The stone says: and the village paid one-eighth.
